# Persuasive writing

All writing is persuasive. It affects what the reader notices, understands, trusts, or chooses. Apply this guidance alongside the existing editing guidance for every writing task, with effort proportionate to the task.

Start by identifying what should change in the reader's understanding, judgment, or ability to act. A tutorial makes a mechanism comprehensible; an explanation reveals an assumption; a proposal gives the reader reasons to choose.

## Begin with the reader

Understand what the reader already knows, believes, values, and doubts. Identify the experience or problem that gives them a reason to care. A technically sound argument can miss its audience when it answers a question they do not have or dismisses something they value.

Infer the audience and purpose from the request and existing material. For substantial writing, briefly state consequential or uncertain framing assumptions in chat so the user can challenge them; keep that discussion outside the prose. Ask a focused question when competing interpretations would materially change the argument and the available context cannot resolve them. Skip this framing step for routine replies and small edits.

Consider what they need to accept before your conclusion becomes useful. A skeptical peer may need the mechanism and tradeoffs. A reader under interruption may need the recommendation immediately, with its essential support on the page. Someone learning a tool may need a successful example before the general explanation.

## Give the piece a purpose

Choose the idea that makes the writing worth reading. It might explain why a system behaves unexpectedly, expose the cost of a familiar practice, reinterpret an event, or recommend a course of action.

Organize around that purpose. Connect facts by explaining how each develops the idea. Select an opening that helps this reader enter the argument: a conclusion, a concrete problem, an observation, or an experience that makes the stakes legible. Follow it with substance that earns their attention.

A provocative claim needs support. A story needs to lead somewhere. An explicit request is appropriate when the reader has a decision to make.

## Make the reasoning visible

Let the reader follow how you reach the conclusion. Explain the mechanism, show the relevant experience, or connect the observation to its consequence. Keep the premises the conclusion depends on visible.

Different forms of support do different work. Measurements establish magnitude within their scope. Examples make a mechanism concrete. Experience can reveal consequences that an abstract model misses. History explains how a situation arose. Analogy helps a reader understand an unfamiliar relationship. Values explain why one tradeoff matters more than another.

Use the support the claim needs and acknowledge what it cannot establish. An anecdote can illustrate a failure without proving its frequency. An analogy can clarify a relationship without proving that two systems behave alike. Separate observations, assumptions, and judgments where a reasonable disagreement could turn on the distinction.

Aim for an argument the reader can examine and challenge. Make clear which premise, value, or tradeoff someone would need to disagree with to reach a different conclusion.

## Take resistance seriously

When a credible alternative or objection affects the reader's judgment, address it. Explain what the alternative does well, why someone would choose it, and where your recommendation differs. Answer the objection that could change the decision.

Understand the attachment behind a position. People may be defending useful capabilities, hard-earned knowledge, or work they are proud of. Acknowledge that substance before explaining why circumstances call for a change. Respect helps the reader consider an argument without having to deny their experience.

When the answer depends on priorities, expose the choice. State which costs or risks you are willing to accept and why. Preserve meaningful uncertainty and the conditions under which you would change your mind.

## Put a person behind the argument

When your motivation or experience helps the reader understand the argument, show why you care and how you came to know the problem. An admitted mistake or a clear value can establish credibility and make the stakes tangible.

Use first and second person when they clarify responsibility, experience, or the reader's task. Third person can serve history and explanation. Choose the perspective that makes the reasoning easiest to follow.

Conviction should come from the substance. Keep the author's voice while improving the argument; stronger adjectives cannot repair a missing premise.

## Choose the shape that serves the purpose

In [The Control Paradox](https://matt.kotsenas.com/posts/the-control-paradox/), a familiar engineering instinct is reconsidered through a concrete failure and the author's own experience. In [the PowerShell startup tutorial](https://matt.kotsenas.com/posts/pwsh-profiling-async-startup/), measured diagnosis and a worked implementation preserve customizations the reader values. Both give the reader a practical way forward.

Cantrill's [unikernel essay](https://bcantrill.dtrace.org/2016/01/22/unikernels-are-unfit-for-production/) changes the evaluation criteria by explaining production debugging consequences. His [Solaris essay](https://bcantrill.dtrace.org/2017/09/04/the-sudden-death-and-eternal-life-of-solaris/) uses history and human loss to explain what open source can preserve. His [compensation essay](https://bcantrill.dtrace.org/2021/03/03/compensation-as-a-reflection-of-values/) makes an organizational choice intelligible through values, commitments, and objections.

Borrow a structure when it serves the present task. Choose it for the reader and the reasoning, and adapt it as the material develops.

## Earn the space on the page

Include enough substance for the reader to judge the argument without reconstructing missing context. Explain the important mechanism, cost, or alternative on the page. Links can provide depth and provenance; the essential reasoning belongs in the text.

Retain detail that earns understanding or confidence. Remove ceremony, repeated setup, and explanations of what the document is about to do. Read each paragraph for what it contributes to the reader's progress.

Write for someone who was absent from the original conversation and may encounter the document later. Supply the context they need without repeating what the text has already established.

Finish the work the piece set out to do. Leave the choice and next action clear in a recommendation; enable the reader to try the technique and interpret the result in a tutorial; give them an understanding they can use in an essay.
